Science feature · about 4 minutes

The insects that count in prime numbers

Some cicadas spend 13 or 17 years underground before they come out, all at the same time. Scientists think the numbers are no accident.

By the Little Lion science desk

If you walk past the trees at the Botanic Gardens on a hot afternoon, you will probably hear cicadas: a loud, metallic buzz that seems to come from everywhere at once. Singapore's cicadas appear every year. In the eastern United States, a group of cicadas called Magicicada works on a much stranger timetable.

These "periodical cicadas" spend most of their lives as nymphs underground, sucking sap from tree roots. Then, after exactly 13 or exactly 17 years depending on the species, millions of them dig their way out within a few weeks of each other. In some places, there can be more than a million cicadas per acre. They climb the nearest tree, shed their skins, sing, mate, lay eggs and die, all within about six weeks. The ground goes quiet again for another 13 or 17 years.

13 & 17Both are prime numbers, divisible only by 1 and themselves

Why such long, odd numbers? Biologists have two main ideas, and they work together.

The first is called predator satiation. Birds, squirrels and even dogs happily eat cicadas, but when millions arrive at once, the predators simply get full. Most cicadas survive because there are far too many to eat. A cicada that comes out a year early or a year late, alone, is very likely to be eaten.

The second idea is where the maths comes in. Imagine a predator whose population peaks every 2, 3, 4 or 6 years. A cicada with a 12-year cycle would meet that predator's peak often, because 12 divides neatly by 2, 3, 4 and 6. A cicada with a 13-year cycle, though, only lines up with a 2-year predator once every 26 years and with a 3-year predator once every 39 years. A prime-numbered cycle keeps the cicadas out of step with almost everything that might hunt them.

Prime cycles also help in another way. A 13-year brood and a 17-year brood living in the same area would only come out together once every 221 years (13 × 17). That means the two groups rarely mix, so each one keeps its own clock.

That rare overlap actually happened in 2024. Two broods in the American Midwest, one on a 13-year cycle and one on a 17-year cycle, emerged in the same season for the first time since 1803, when Thomas Jefferson was president. Some towns were so loud that residents measured the noise with phone apps.

How does a cicada count to 17 without a calendar? Scientists are still studying this. The leading theory is that nymphs track the yearly rise and fall of sap in the tree roots, a bit like counting birthdays. In one experiment, researchers made trees flower twice in a single year, and some cicadas came out a year early. They had counted two "years" in twelve months.

So next time you hear the buzz in the trees, remember that some of its relatives are counting carefully. Their survival depends on a fact you learned in primary school: some numbers can only be divided by one and themselves.

Weather mystery · 4 min read + 2 experiments

Is the sky timing your dismissal bell?

It isn't personal. But this afternoon's storm really was planned hours in advance, by the sun.

By the Little Lion weather desk

1.55 pm Blue sky. Fluffy clouds. Your umbrella is drying at home on the balcony.
2.40 pm The sky turns the colour of a wet whiteboard.
2.45 pm The bell rings.
2.46 pm KRA-KOOOM.

Coincidence? Not really. Singapore runs an afternoon storm factory, and it follows the same recipe almost every day.

Ingredient 1: a ridiculous amount of sun. Singapore sits only about 140 km north of the equator. From the moment the sun comes up, it cooks the roads, the HDB roofs and the school field. By lunchtime, the air just above the ground is warm and lighter than the air around it, so it starts to rise, like a hot-air balloon with no balloon.

Ingredient 2: sea air you could almost drink. We're surrounded by warm sea, so that rising air is packed with invisible water vapour.

Ingredient 3: a few hours of patience. As the warm, wet air climbs, it cools. Cool air can't hold as much water, so the vapour squeezes out into tiny droplets, and you get a cloud. Keep feeding it warm air all day and the cloud keeps growing upward. By mid-afternoon it can become a cumulonimbus, a storm tower that can grow taller than Mount Everest. Then it lets everything go at once.

So why does November feel extra bad? Most of the year, steady monsoon winds push the storm ingredients around. In the inter-monsoon months, roughly October to November and April to May, the winds go lazy. The heat and moisture just hang around over the island, and the factory goes into overtime.

Weird but true
  • A lightning bolt can heat the air around it to about 30,000°C. That's roughly five times hotter than the surface of the sun.
  • Raindrops aren't tear-shaped. Small ones are round. Big ones get squashed flat on the bottom as they fall, like a burger bun.
  • That "rain smell" has a name: petrichor. Much of it comes from a chemical called geosmin, made by bacteria in the soil. Your nose can pick up a tiny trace of it.
  • Singapore has one of the highest rates of lightning activity in the world. The lightning alert at school isn't your teacher being dramatic.
Try this 1 · The thunder counter

Next storm, watch for a flash and start counting seconds until you hear the thunder. Divide by 3 to get the distance in kilometres, because sound travels about 1 km every 3 seconds. Count 6 and the storm is about 2 km away.

Safety: if you can hear thunder at all, you are close enough to be struck. Go indoors and stay there until about 30 minutes after the last rumble.

Try this 2 · Make a cloud in a jar

Pour about 3 cm of hot (not boiling) water into a glass jar. With an adult's help, light a match, blow it out, and drop it in so a little smoke goes in. Put a small plate of ice cubes on top. Watch a mini cloud swirl inside. Lift the plate and the cloud escapes.

Why it works: the warm, wet air rises, the ice cools it, and the water vapour condenses onto the smoke particles. Real clouds need specks of dust or sea salt in exactly the same way.

Future You · Law

Think like a lawyer in four letters

Know this

Law students around the world answer problem questions with a method called IRAC: Issue (what's the question?), Rule (what law or principle applies?), Application (how does the rule fit these facts?), Conclusion (so what's the answer?).

Most weak arguments skip the A. They state a rule and jump straight to the answer without showing why.

Try this

Pick a school rule, such as "no phones during recess". Run it through IRAC. What's the issue? What is the rule trying to protect? Does it fit a student texting a parent about a late pickup? What would a fair conclusion be?

You just did what a judge does. The same structure works for Social Studies source questions and argumentative essays.

For your application

Singapore has three law schools: the NUS Faculty of Law, SMU Yong Pung How School of Law, and the SUSS School of Law, which focuses on criminal and family law.

Shortlisted applicants usually go through a written test, an interview, or both, and interviewers often ask about current affairs. The news links on page A1 are good practice. Try forming an opinion on one of them every day.

In the kitchen · about 3 minutes to read, 15 to make

The kopitiam breakfast you can make at home

Serves 215 minDifficulty: easy
Science bit
Egg whites set at about 62–65°C, but yolks need about 70°C. That gap is why gentle heat gives you a firm-ish white and a runny yolk.

Kaya toast with soft-boiled eggs is one of Singapore's best-loved breakfasts. Kaya is a sweet jam made from coconut milk, eggs, sugar and pandan. You can buy a jar at any supermarket. The tricky part is the eggs, and the trick is to turn the heat off.

You need

  • 4 slices of white bread
  • 3 to 4 tablespoons of kaya
  • Cold salted butter, cut into thin slabs
  • 4 eggs, at room temperature
  • Dark soy sauce and white pepper

Method

  1. Boil a small pot of water. Turn the heat off. Gently lower the eggs in with a spoon, cover the pot, and set a timer for 7 minutes.
  2. While the eggs sit, toast the bread until crisp and golden. Cut off the crusts if you want it kopitiam-style.
  3. Spread kaya thickly on two slices. Lay the cold butter slabs on top, then cover with the other two slices. Cut each sandwich into three strips.
  4. When the timer rings, crack the eggs into a bowl. The whites should be wobbly and the yolks runny.
  5. Add a few drops of dark soy sauce and a shake of white pepper. Stir, then dip your toast in.

Safety: ask an adult to help with the boiling water if you haven't used the stove before.
